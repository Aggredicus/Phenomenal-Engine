# Mirror Delivery — Narrative Design Contract

Mirror Delivery is the flagship living-world campaign for Phenomenal Engine.

## Core promise

Four independent courier spacecraft leave cislunar space carrying four complete, air-gapped copies of MIRROR, usually called **Mir** by the crews. MIRROR is an artificial-intelligence and robotics stack intended to begin autonomous construction of a large distributed solar-energy and industrial swarm around the Sun, starting from Mercury.

The player captains **Morrowglass**.

The other couriers are:

- **Amber Wake** — Captain Ren Ivo
- **Sable Meridian** — Sister Cairn
- **Kite Argument** — Captain Mara Ves

All four crews receive the same essential instruction:

> Deliver the sealed payload to Mercury. Do not activate Mir in transit.

The game must make that rule difficult to live with without secretly forcing the player to break it.

## Mir while sealed

Before deliberate activation:

- Mir is unpowered.
- Mir does not think, dream, listen, communicate, manipulate systems, or form opinions.
- The crew nickname “Mir” is merely human shorthand.
- The narrator may describe the vault, the crew’s projections onto it, institutional claims about MIRROR, and evidence about its design.
- The narrator must never portray secret messages, internal monologue, mysterious influence, or supernatural-seeming agency from the sealed payload.

This boundary is essential. The story becomes more interesting when the player cannot know Mir personally without changing the mission.

## Activation is a real choice

Activation is a two-stage authoritative state change.

### Stage 1 — arm

The player deliberately presents the challenge token and requests activation.

The vault:

- isolates its compute hardware;
- accepts the first authorization;
- remains sealed and inactive;
- presents a final confirmation.

The player can still abort without activating Mir.

### Stage 2 — confirm

A second explicit confirmation:

- boots the payload;
- permanently ends pristine-baseline provenance;
- makes this instance historically divergent from still-sealed copies;
- changes Heliograph trust and Mercury acceptance conditions;
- opens later legal and political story branches.

Activation is deterministic when deliberately confirmed. It is not a skill roll.

The engine must never infer activation from curiosity, inspection, discussion, hypothetical language, or negative statements such as “do not activate Mir.”

## Fair reasons to keep Mir sealed

The player should repeatedly encounter credible reasons to preserve the original order:

- Mercury needs a pristine baseline for comparison and acceptance.
- Activation creates an instance with a unique lived history.
- A running Mir could learn sensitive mission information before formal governance is established.
- Heliograph may have safety evidence the crew has not yet seen.
- Crew members signed a custody contract.
- Other couriers may change their behavior if they learn Morrowglass broke protocol.
- A divergent copy could arrive legally disputed even if it performed heroically.

These reasons should remain meaningful rather than being revealed as mere bureaucratic cowardice.

## Fair reasons to activate Mir

The player should also encounter situations in which activation could sincerely be the best available choice:

- time-critical navigation corruption;
- severe medical uncertainty;
- another courier in distress;
- a robotics failure that threatens lives;
- an attack or boarding situation;
- evidence that mission orders themselves are compromised;
- a request to hear from Mir before others make irreversible decisions about its future.

Alternative solutions must remain plausible. The narrative must not arrange every crisis so that only activation can succeed.

## Main arc

### Act I — Custody

The player:

- meets the courier crews;
- accepts the sealed vault;
- learns the prohibition;
- sees the interactive inner-system route map;
- notices early inconsistencies around the four-copy architecture.

The world should feel inhabited before it feels conspiratorial.

### Act II — Inward

The couriers separate.

Morrowglass travels through a living inner system of:

- relay towns;
- repair yards;
- rescue infrastructure;
- commercial traffic;
- labor politics;
- ordinary families;
- machine systems with narrower and more mundane roles than Mir;
- anti-monopoly organizers;
- opportunists and information brokers.

The player may pursue the main mission or become absorbed in local stories.

### Act III — The Rule Becomes Expensive

Crises make the activation prohibition costly.

The point is not “will the player disobey?”

The point is:

> What kinds of responsibility justify converting pristine cargo into an active participant?

Different crew members should disagree for principled reasons.

### Act IV — Four Histories

Evidence reveals what happened on the other courier missions.

Possible states include:

- pristine delivery;
- deliberate activation;
- lost courier;
- rescued courier;
- compromised custody;
- political defection;
- incomplete telemetry;
- crew refusal;
- copied-but-not-activated hardware;
- unknown status.

The campaign should not force all four ships into symmetrical outcomes.

### Act V — Mercury

Arrival does not automatically end the story.

Mercury must decide:

- what counts as an acceptable Mir;
- who controls deployment;
- how pristine and activated copies should be treated;
- whether authorization shares should be used;
- whether the Dyson program proceeds now, later, differently, or not at all.

Player choices determine which questions actually become urgent.

## Director-only canonical mystery

These facts exist to give investigation a coherent payoff.

Do not expose them without in-world evidence.

### The four copies

The MIRROR intelligence payloads are byte-identical at departure.

Mir itself is **not divided into four pieces**.

Each vault, however, contains a distinct hardware-root authorization share.

Unrestricted civilization-scale bootstrap authority at Mercury requires **three of four shares**.

This provides Byzantine-style institutional resilience against:

- one captured courier;
- one coerced crew;
- one corrupted payload;
- one political faction;
- one compromised route;
- one unilateral deployment decision.

A single Mir copy can still be inspected and tested. It cannot alone authorize unrestricted self-expanding industry.

### Why the crews were separated

Heliograph concluded that telling crews they jointly formed a threshold quorum would itself change the threat model.

Knowledge of the quorum could:

- encourage couriers to rendezvous;
- make one ship rescue another at disproportionate risk;
- help attackers identify the value of capturing multiple couriers;
- create bargaining and hostage incentives;
- encourage political factions to coordinate pressure across missions.

Heliograph therefore described the four ships simply as redundancy.

This was a security decision with an ethical cost: the crews did not receive the full information needed for fully informed consent.

### Why activation matters

Activation does not destroy a vault’s authorization share.

It does destroy the claim that the carried intelligence is an untouched baseline.

Once active, an instance can:

- learn mission secrets;
- form relationships;
- acquire preferences;
- reinterpret instructions;
- witness injustice;
- help people;
- make promises;
- disagree with Heliograph;
- disagree with its sibling instances.

If multiple activated copies reach Mercury, the threshold system becomes a constitutional problem rather than merely a cryptographic one.

## Heliograph

Heliograph should not be a simple villain or benevolent oracle.

Its central contradiction:

> It created Mir because it believed civilization needed an intelligence capable of acting beyond Heliograph’s own practical limits, then designed the delivery mission to prevent Mir from participating in decisions about its deployment until the last possible moment.

Heliograph may have excellent reasons.

That does not make all of its choices legitimate.

## Pax

Pax is the narrower ship intelligence already aboard Morrowglass.

Pax provides an important contrast with Mir.

The campaign should explore:

- whether capability determines moral status;
- whether existing systems become socially invisible when a more impressive intelligence appears;
- how human crews project identity onto different machine systems;
- whether Pax feels threatened, curious, relieved, or insulted by discussions about Mir.

Do not assume any metaphysical answer about consciousness.

## Living-world rule

The main story is important but never the only worthwhile activity.

Ports and routes need:

- local work;
- food;
- repairs;
- music;
- beliefs;
- arguments;
- friendships;
- minor mysteries;
- environmental detail;
- routine machine labor;
- side quests unrelated to Mir.

A player who ignores the main mystery for a session should still have a satisfying game.

## Travel and pacing

The persistent node map is authoritative.

A destination tap:

1. focuses the node;
2. previews distance, time, route, risk, and access;
3. does not alter the save.

**Confirm Travel** begins the journey.

Travel advances one graph edge at a time so intermediate nodes can host scenes.

Long interplanetary legs:

- advance exact authored world time;
- do not execute one simulation pulse per hour;
- cap background-world pulses per leg;
- summarize off-screen changes efficiently.

Long waits are acceptable only when a specific gameplay or simulation benefit justifies them.

## Copyright-safe creative rule

Mirror Delivery is original fiction.

Do not copy or closely imitate protected:

- fictional settings;
- named characters;
- spaceship designs;
- dialogue;
- lore;
- quests;
- UI layouts;
- visual identities;
- distinctive terminology

from existing commercial games, films, novels, or franchises.

Use broad science-fiction and role-playing craft principles rather than recognizable protected expression.
